Graphs over time

Speed History

Dots are raw GPS fixes; the line is smoothed between them. Long flat stretches at zero are stops — compare their length to the moving stretches to see how much of the trip was waiting.

Altitude Profile

A real hill is a slow, rounded rise. Sharp single-point spikes are GPS altitude noise — the gain/loss totals filter those out, so trust the totals more than any one spike.

Acceleration History

Above the line is speeding up, below is braking. Because fixes arrive ~30 s apart, this is a coarse average between fixes — a tall narrow spike usually means a short hard brake, not a data error.

Distance Over Time

The slope of this line IS your speed: steep means fast, flat means stopped. A kink in the slope is where the drive changed character — a light, a turn, a new road.

Vertical Speed History

Positive is climbing, negative is descending. Sustained values of a few feet per second are real grades; rapid flips between positive and negative are altitude noise, not the road.

Heading Over Time

Flat sections are straight roads; smooth ramps are curves; near-vertical jumps are turns. A heading that wanders while speed is zero is GPS drift in the garage, not the car moving.

Elevation Profile vs Distance

This is the road's true shape: distance along the bottom, height on the side. The steepest climbs are where the grade is worst — overlay speed to see exactly how much each hill cost you.

Cumulative Elevation Gain / Loss

Two running totals: everything climbed and everything descended. On a loop drive they should nearly match; a big gap means the trip ended much higher — or lower — than it started.

GPS Accuracy Over Time

Lower is better: under 10 m is a good fix, over 50 m is a rough one. Accuracy usually worsens in parking garages, between tall buildings, and under tree cover — exactly where the trail also gets jumpy.

The arc sweeps 0–120. A needle that climbs in smooth steps means steady throttle; a needle that jumps between fixes is the Tesla browser's ~30-second GPS rhythm, not your driving.

Altitude Gauge

—

GPS altitude in the Tesla browser is often null or jumpy — a flat needle with a dash means the car is not reporting elevation at all. When it works, watch it drift slowly on hills, never in sharp steps.

G-Force Circle

Longitudinal g (brake/accelerate) runs left–right, lateral g (cornering) runs up–down — the lateral value is derived from how fast your heading changes times your speed. A dot near the edge is a hard maneuver; a tight cluster near the center is a calm drive.

Compass Rose

—

The needle points the way the car is travelling, not the way it is parked. If it spins while you are stopped, that is GPS heading noise — the compass only means something above a few mph.

A self-drawn trail — no map tiles, no internet needed. Straight segments between fixes are the honest picture: with ~30-second fixes, every curve is really a series of short straight chords.

Speed Histogram

Where the bars pile up is your cruising speed. A tall bar at zero means lots of stopped time; a wide spread means mixed city driving; a narrow peak means steady highway cruising.

Altitude Histogram

This shows which elevations you spent the most time at. Two separated humps usually mean two different areas — a valley and a plateau — rather than one rolling road.

Acceleration Histogram

A symmetric hump around zero is a smooth driver. A long tail to the left means repeated hard braking; a long tail to the right means repeated hard launches. The shape is your driving style in one picture.

Speed Bands — Time Spent

Time in each speed band, not distance. If the 0–20 band dominates a "highway" trip, you spent the trip in traffic or at lights — the band split tells the truth the average speed hides.

Trip Statistics

The trip in numbers. Moving time excludes stops, so average moving speed is the honest measure of the roads — compare it with overall average speed to see how much stopping cost you.

Honest limits in the Tesla browser: GPS updates arrive roughly once every 30 seconds and positions are quantized, so speed, acceleration and g-force are derived from those sparse fixes — smooth in the simulator, steppy in the real car. GPS altitude is frequently null. The browser exposes no gyroscope and DeviceMotionEvent fires nothing here, so there is no true accelerometer feed.
